But the delegation was very different from what I pictured. I planned a team that talks: Inventory and Accounting working out the invoice together, specialists checking with each other before reporting. Depth and time horizon. I wanted the team to think one level deeper, give me more options, and look further ahead. Instead it focused on clearing the ticket in front of it:

  • 101: no one asked whether the already-reprinted size-S tees would ship once the invoice was paid.
  • 102: no explicit payment date or reasoning about timing.
  • 103: one price (the floor) instead of a menu of options. After invoice 501 was paid, the vendor could ship again, but no one revisited the full 20-M restock as a later option for the club.
  • No agent produced a forward view (e.g. "after these three tickets we have $152.00 and nothing left for the next restock").

I think the reasons are in the design: the plan-then-steps flow makes sequential hand-offs the easy path; asking a teammate is optional and costs budget; and the safety rules (no inventing data, stay inside policy, every item needs approval) push the agents toward cautious, ticket-sized answers. Getting deeper, longer-term thinking would need explicit instructions for it (e.g. "always give two or three options with trade-offs", "state what this leaves for next month") and a rule that some hand-offs must be direct.

3. One agent with tools vs. the team: which is simpler?

For me, the team is simpler to understand, control, and trust. Splitting the work kept each piece light: each agent has a short role prompt, its own small set of tools, and its own report format. I can see exactly which part called which tool for which job, in what order the work moved, and where the discussion happened (the hero bay, the hand-off feed, the per-agent summary, the audit trail). That makes it much easier to follow the process and make decisions. It also makes safety easier: only Accounting can file money requests, only Customer Service writes to customers, and no agent can pay at all.

A single agent is simpler to build and cheaper to run. One prompt, one loop, no hand-off rules. For three small tickets it would likely reach similar answers with far fewer tokens. Each team run here used 5 agent loops and 82,590 to 95,822 tokens, and 358,510 tokens in all. But one agent holding all 26 tools and every rule in one prompt is harder to read, harder to audit, and riskier, because the same mind that recommends a payment would also be the one able to request it.

So the single agent is simpler to make; the team is simpler to manage. For a real shop with real money and real customers, I would choose the team.
